Your teams are already asking their AI assistants project questions — how long will this take, what does it really cost, are we on track. The assistants answer from general knowledge, because your organisation's own decision logic is not where they can reach it. This briefing covers a small experiment we ran to close that gap, and what it settles for anyone weighing the same move.

## Key Judgements: The barrier to putting a decision tool where your AI can reach it is now an engineering week, not a platform programme

- Serving a decision tool to AI clients over remote MCP is a **small, verifiable engineering exercise** — we validated the pattern with a live deployment of one estimation tool, built without any vendor framework.
- The risk floor is set by **design choices you can inspect**, not vendor promises: our proof of concept stores nothing, logs no request content, and refuses to serve at all until its access credential exists.
- The real cost sits in **identity and access, not compute**. Connecting developer-grade clients is trivial; reaching consumer AI surfaces requires an OAuth investment that deserves its own decision.

## Situation: A tool on one laptop helps one person; the question was what a team endpoint actually costs

Our open-source toolkit ships as a local server — one command (`uvx pmorun-mcp`) puts calibrated estimation maths inside a single person's AI assistant. That is deliberate: local means private, and installation is the only barrier. But value that lives on one laptop does not compound. The Model Context Protocol's remote variant turns the same tools into a URL that every assistant in the organisation can call — and the industry is moving that way quickly. Before recommending the pattern to anyone, we wanted first-hand evidence of what the move costs, so we built and deployed a proof of concept.

## Analysis: One estimator, ported and parity-tested, served from a stateless edge worker — with access failing closed

The experiment: take our PERT estimation tool and serve it over remote MCP from a serverless edge function (Cloudflare Workers). The results, all verifiable in [the public repository](https://github.com/lemur47/logic/tree/main/examples/remote-mcp):

- The whole server is roughly **330 lines of TypeScript with no framework dependency** — small enough for one engineer to audit in an afternoon.
- The port is **proven equivalent to the original**: an automated suite checks it against fixtures generated by the Python module, so the maths cannot silently drift.
- It is **stateless by design** — no storage, no persistence, no request-content logging — so there is no data at rest to govern.
- Access **fails closed**: until an access token is configured, the endpoint returns "service unavailable" rather than serving anyone. A misconfiguration produces an outage, not an exposure.

## Evaluation: Statelessness makes the privacy posture easy to defend; the deliberate omissions mark where a pilot ends and a product begins

For a decision-maker, the significant finding is what the proof of concept did *not* need: no platform procurement, no data-protection review of stored content (there is none), no standing infrastructure cost worth naming. The omissions are equally informative, because they mark the pilot-to-product boundary. We left out the OAuth flow that consumer AI surfaces require, any persistence (which would immediately create a data-governance surface), and any service commitment — this is a validation probe, not a product. One architectural question the experiment opened rather than closed: the same core logic could serve both MCP and a conventional REST interface, and whether that dual surface is worth its maintenance cost is something we are still exploring.

## Recommendation: Pilot remote MCP with developer-grade clients now; treat identity and persistence as separate, deliberate decisions

If your organisation wants its own numbers inside its AI conversations, the evidence supports a low-commitment sequence: pilot with header-capable clients (developer tooling such as Claude Code connects today with a generated token), keep the pilot stateless so no new data surface appears, and add rate limiting at the route. Hold consumer AI surfaces — and anything requiring user accounts or stored state — for a second, deliberate decision once the pilot shows usage. The pattern is open source and self-hostable; the only secret involved is one you generate yourself.
